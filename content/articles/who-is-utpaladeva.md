Utpaladeva lived in Kashmir in the tenth century. He was the student of Somānanda, whose *Śivadṛṣṭi* argued that the whole of reality is Śiva, and the teacher of Lakṣmaṇagupta, who in turn taught Abhinavagupta.[^1] Through that line, Utpala's arguments became the philosophical spine of what later readers call Kashmir Śaivism.

His major work, the *Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā*, the “Verses on the Recognition of the Lord”, makes a striking wager: liberation is not gaining something new but recognising (*pratyabhijñā*) what one already is. He also wrote two commentaries on his own verses, a short *Vṛtti* and a long *Vivṛti*, of which only fragments survive.[^2]

> ĪPK 1.1.1
>
> *kathaṃcid āsādya maheśvarasya dāsyaṃ janasyāpy upakāram icchan |*
> *samastasampatsamavāptihetuṃ tatpratyabhijñām upapādayāmi ||*
>
> Having somehow attained the service of the great Lord, and wishing to help others too, I set out the recognition of Him: the cause of attaining every fulfilment.

What makes Utpala unusual is that he argues for this recognition rather than simply announcing it. The *Kārikā* meets the Buddhist epistemologists on their own ground, memory, concepts, causation and relation, to show that none of them can be explained without a single, self-aware subject.

<p class="pullquote">“Liberation is not gaining something new, but recognising what one already is.”</p>

He was also a poet. The *Śivastotrāvalī*, a collection of his devotional hymns, shows the same recognition from the inside, as longing and delight rather than proof.

[^1]: Abhinavagupta wrote two commentaries on Utpala's work: the *Vimarśinī* on the verses and the *Vivṛtivimarśinī* on the long commentary.

[^2]: The *Vivṛti* is known mainly through Abhinavagupta's commentary on it. [Reference]
